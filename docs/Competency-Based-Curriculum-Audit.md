# Competency-Based Curriculum Audit and Design Basis

**Author:** Manus AI  
**Scope:** Crop Advisor Training, Modules 01–34  
**Status:** Design basis for the competency-based enhancement.

## Audit conclusion

The existing course already contains important competency-based assets: sequential modules, 80% formal knowledge checks, applied field briefs, scenarios, field records, measurement routines, a practicum, capstones, portfolio evidence, and safety/referral boundaries. The principal gap is visibility and coherence. Learners can encounter authentic practice, but the course does not yet state, for every module, the observable field competency, the evidence a learner should retain, the criteria by which that evidence is judged, and the remediation route when performance is incomplete.

UNESCO-UNEVOC describes competency-based education as learners demonstrating knowledge, skills, and attitudes in specific units, usually in authentic contexts; it also emphasises actual demonstration, awareness of the standard, and progression by attained competencies rather than exposure alone.[1] The redesign therefore retains the current scored checks but makes their applied purpose explicit: each module now requires a clear field-performance target, evidence, criteria, review/referral boundary, and a targeted practice route.

| Current strength | Competency-based adjustment | Result for learners |
|---|---|---|
| Two lessons and an 80% module check | Add an explicit module competency and criterion-referenced performance task. | Learners know what capable field performance looks like, not only what to revise for a quiz. |
| Applied briefs, scenarios, records, routines, practicum, and capstones | Map each asset to the competency it evidences and show a remediation route. | Practice becomes purposeful evidence rather than an optional extra. |
| Sequential gates and certification | Keep formal gates unchanged; treat applied evidence as developmental field-readiness evidence. | Existing certification safeguards remain intact while readiness becomes more visible. |
| Strong crop-protection safety boundaries | Repeat task-specific uncertainty, referral, label, laboratory, specialist, and authorised-channel boundaries. | Competence includes knowing when not to prescribe or proceed. |

## Framework architecture

The course is organised into eight competency domains: advisory judgement; site, soil, and crop-performance analysis; planning and economics; soil and nutrient stewardship; nursery and establishment; water and irrigation; crop care and market quality; and crop health/IPM/pesticide stewardship. This grouping aligns the course’s agronomic content with broader agricultural advisory work, where advisory services support producers’ productivity, profitability, risk management, and informed decisions rather than only technical input selection.[2] It also remains broadly compatible with established crop-advisory competency areas that group soil and water, nutrient, pest, and crop management.[3]

Every module is mapped to one primary observable competency and uses the same three performance levels:

| Level | Learner evidence |
|---|---|
| **Prepare** | Frames the task, method, evidence needs, and safety conditions. |
| **Perform** | Completes an observable field task or decision sequence using traceable evidence. |
| **Review and refer** | Defines the outcome check, uncertainty, and the condition to revise, stop, or refer. |

The framework does **not** convert all learning into independent practice or remove instruction. It combines explicit learning, an 80% criterion-referenced knowledge check, and contextual performance evidence. The New Extensionist literature likewise identifies agricultural advisory work as a set of roles and capacities within a wider innovation system, supporting the inclusion of communication, local intelligence, ethics, and review alongside technical agronomy.[4]

## Assessment and safety boundaries

Formal module checks continue to require 80%. They remain the current sequential progression mechanism. The new competency evidence is learner-facing and developmental: it can be assembled in existing records, routines, annotation practice, practicum visits, scenarios, capstones, and the portfolio. It does not change certification, owner alerts, assessment scores, or introduce unsupported local thresholds.

For high-consequence crop protection, competence is explicitly bounded. Learners must recognise uncertainty, stop unsafe work, retain evidence, check the current label and authorised channels, use laboratory or specialist support where needed, and avoid improvised medical, cleanup, disposal, legal-reporting, product, rate, threshold, or emergency instructions.
