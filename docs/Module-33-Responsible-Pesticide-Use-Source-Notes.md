# Module 33 Source Notes: Responsible Use of Pesticides

## Source

- PDF: `REVISED_PRT-005.ResponsibleUseofPesticides_06022023.pdf`

## Key findings captured from pages 1–5

The document title is **Responsible Use of Pesticides: Good Practices to Minimize Health and Environmental Risks**. Its learning objectives state that participants should learn the importance of reading and understanding pesticide labels, describe and demonstrate the proper handling and mixing of pesticides, and present to farmers the do’s and don’ts when spraying pesticides while explaining the possible consequences of mishandling.

The outline presents six instructional blocks: golden rules when using pesticides, what is in the label, how to protect yourself when handling pesticides, use the correct product, the principle that the best product is only as good as its application, and general knowledge about pesticides.

The visible definition slide states that a pesticide is something that prevents, destroys, or controls a harmful organism or disease, or protects plants or plant products during production, storage, and transport.

The visible pesticide-types table includes fungicides for fungi, insecticides for insects, herbicides for weeds, nematicides for nematodes, rodenticides for rodents, acaricides or miticides for spiders or mites, molluscicides for snails and slugs, bactericides for bacteria, algicides for algae, and avicides for birds. This supports a source-grounded distinction between target organism and pesticide type.

The **five golden rules when using pesticides** shown in the source are to read and understand the label, handle pesticides carefully at all times, maintain sprayers, practice good personal hygiene, and use protective clothing when spraying.

The label-reading slide lists specific information learners should check before use: which crop is recommended, which pests it will control, environmental precautions, dose and frequency of use, pre-harvest interval (PHI), active ingredient, storage advice, re-entry time, safety advice, hazard class, expiry date, and mode of action. A visible annotation states that newer labels include the mode-of-action group.

The hazard-code slides show a **color bar and pictograms** system. The visible bars indicate 1A extremely hazardous, 1B highly hazardous, 2 moderately hazardous, 3 slightly hazardous, and U acute hazard unlikely. The source also shows where color bars are found on labels.

The pictogram slides connect label symbols with practical actions. Visible examples include wearing a long-sleeved shirt and long trousers when spraying, using gloves when handling concentrated chemicals and spraying, using a face shield, and wearing boots. Additional symbols shown indicate harmful products, harm to animals and aquatic life, washing hands after use, and keeping chemicals locked up and out of reach of children.

The self-protection slide states that different parts of the body vary in their ability to absorb pesticides, urges learners to stop spraying immediately if they have fatigue, headache, nausea, or dizziness, and instructs them not to allow themselves or others to become dangerously ill before seeking medical assistance.

The measurement slide says users should use proper tools to measure the right quantity because measuring accurately is very important. It warns that too much gives higher cost, too low gives poor control, and measuring devices used for pesticides should be cleaned thoroughly after use and not used to prepare food.

The “Can you spot them?” examples identify unsafe behaviour in field spraying. The visible points include not wearing required personal protective equipment such as a mask or gloves, working close to a water source where contamination may occur, high pouring of water from a bucket that can cause splashes, and not wearing boots to protect feet and legs.

The safety-procedures slide for handling pesticides during mixing and measuring tells learners to always wear gloves and a mask, avoid dust and splashing, use a stick for mixing, replace the pesticide-lid immediately, triple-rinse containers once empty and add the rinse water to the spray tank to use on the main crop or border crops, and dispose of empty packaging carefully. A highlighted warning states never to mix or fill sprayers close to water sources to avoid contamination.

The sprayer-check slide instructs learners to check sprayers for leaks with clean water before use, calibrate the sprayer output at least once per season, ensure even and uniform application by checking the nozzle, clean the sprayer after each use, and replace nozzles frequently.

## Preliminary implications for Module 33

The first lesson can likely focus on label literacy, hazard classes, pictograms, target-product fit, and the golden rules for responsible pesticide handling. The second lesson can likely focus on personal protection, safe mixing and measuring, contamination prevention, sprayer maintenance and calibration, field do’s and don’ts, and the principle that product performance depends on correct and safe application.

## Additional findings captured from pages 6–10

The next slides expand **safety precautions and hygiene**. Visible guidance says to always have clean water available, wash hands before eating, drinking, smoking, or urinating, wash hands after spraying or handling pesticides, wash clothes separately from domestic washing, and rinse splashes immediately. A boxed “do not” list warns learners not to eat, smoke, or drink while handling pesticides, not to spray when feeling unwell, not to spray in hot weather, and not to clear nozzles by blowing.

The application slides emphasise that **the best product is only as good as its application**. One slide presents spray effectiveness as a combination of product quality and how the product is applied, and warns that every drop not reaching its target is not economic, is lost for pest control, and affects health and the environment.

The factors affecting spray application listed in the source are rain, wind, humidity, temperature, water quality, pest life cycle, state of equipment, operator and technique, crop-development stage, and the way of application. One visible example states that spraying should be directed to the underside of leaves when the pest is present there.

The weather-conditions slide gives practical timing rules. Cooler early morning or late afternoon conditions are presented as best for temperature; low temperature improves efficacy while high temperature can damage the crop and increase droplet evaporation. For humidity, the source advises not spraying at high-humidity levels and positions early morning or late afternoon as suitable times. For wind, the best condition is a light breeze when leaves move gently and smoke shows wind direction, and the slide explicitly warns not to spray if rain is expected within two to three hours and not to spray when windy.

The coverage slides explain that **good coverage is essential**. They note that most insects are active on leaves during cooler times, that many pests and diseases occur on leaf undersides, that preventive fungicides and other contact products must cover the entire leaf surface, that the underside of the leaf often has more stomata and a thinner cuticle, and that pesticide can be better absorbed through the leaf underside while sunlight and rain protection can be better there.

The droplet and nozzle slides link droplet size, pressure, and drift risk. Smaller drops are shown as giving better coverage, while higher pressure produces smaller drops; at the same time, the slide warns that small drops drift. The nozzle-choice slide links chemical type and nozzle pattern: fungicide with flat fan or hollow cone, herbicide with reflex nozzle or flat fan, and insecticide with hollow cone or flat fan.

The nozzle slide states that nozzle choice affects coverage of chemical on the surface, the amount of chemical applied, spray uniformity, and drift. It also advises keeping the nozzle at a fixed height and moving it as steadily as possible.

The adjuvant slide distinguishes **spreader** and **sticker** functions. A spreader can help coverage, reduce surface tension as a detergent-like aid, and help when leaves are waxy or hairy, but can run off with excess use. A sticker can help coverage by improving adhesion to the surface, reducing evaporation of pesticide, and improving waterproofing or rainfastness when no soluble sticker is used.

The calibration slides state that calibration assures effective pest control, lower cost, and improved safety. The source lists walking speed, spray pressure, lance height, nozzle choice, and crop growth stage as factors affecting sprayer output. It warns that incorrect results can cause dangerous residues, resurgence, health and environmental risks, product failure, and wasted money. One visible benchmark states that if a farmer uses a quantity between 200 and 240 litres of spray solution per acre, it is normal, while more than 400 litres per acre suggests the spraying method should be reviewed.

The product-choice slide says that when choosing between formulations, users should consider registration, which product will be most effective against the target pest, which product will have less impact on beneficial insects, and which is safest for user and consumer. A note on less-toxic products says they are safer to use, maintain beneficial-insect balance, are more effective in pest control, and often have immediate effect but not instant kill.

The formulation slides compare several common forms. **Water-dispersible granule (WG)** is described as a spray mix requiring constant agitation and easier to measure and mix than wettable powder. **Emulsifiable concentrate (EC)** is described as higher concentration, more hazardous, easily mixed, able to cause leaf burn, and easily absorbed through skin. **Wettable powder (WP)** is described as less skin absorption than EC, hazardous if inhaled and often dusty, and needing pre-mixing and constant agitation.

The tank-mix-order slide says to mix only chemicals known to be compatible and instructs learners to mix the solid first, then the liquid. The visible order is water-dispersible granules, wettable powders, suspension concentrate, emulsifiable concentrate, soluble concentrate, foliar fertilizers, and then adjuvants.

The visible mechanism-of-action slides distinguish **systemic pesticides**, which are translocated inside the plant and can protect young growth and work for sucking insects and borers; **contact pesticides**, which kill where the drop lands and do not move in the plant; **stomach pesticides**, which must be swallowed by the insect and can be selective; and **repellents**, which deter pests from approaching or settling on the plant.

## Updated implications for Module 33

The module should show that responsible pesticide use is not only a matter of selecting a legal product; it also depends on reading the label, choosing the right formulation and nozzle, mixing safely and compatibly, applying at the right time and coverage, calibrating the sprayer, protecting beneficials and people, and preventing drift, residues, and contamination.

## Final findings captured from pages 11–13

The insecticide mode-of-action slide illustrates several modes through how the target is affected: direct contact, secondary contact, ingestion, repellence, fumigant action, lure-and-kill, and a combination where a modifier improves absorption by the insect. The module context should retain the decision principle rather than treat graphics as a universal product-selection guide.

The pesticide-content slide distinguishes **active ingredient** from **inert ingredients**. Active ingredient is presented as the component that controls pest or disease, while inert ingredients include functions such as carrier, anti-foam, sticker, spreader, emulsifier, fragrance, and formulation support. This distinction is reinforced in a slide comparing active ingredient with mode of action.

The active-ingredient versus mode-of-action slide states that active ingredients are chemicals in pesticides that control or repel pests, while mode of action describes the functional way a pesticide controls or repels a particular target, such as through a nervous-system or physiological process. The source points learners to herbicide, fungicide, and insecticide mode-of-action resources.

The fungicide slide distinguishes **preventive** and **curative** roles. Preventive fungicides prevent fungal infection and must be used before infection; the source notes they do not kill fungus already inside the plant. Curative fungicides kill fungi that have already infected the plant and are usually systemic. The slide also notes that copper-based fungicides are protective and can control some bacterial diseases, and illustrates repeated application spacing as an example rather than a universal programme.

The pesticide-resistance slides show the relationship between trade name, active ingredient, and mode of action. The source’s prevention list includes using non-chemical methods, applying only when necessary, applying selective pesticides rather than broad-spectrum products, using the advised rate rather than too little or too much, avoiding mixing pesticides, alternating pesticide products from different mode-of-action groups, and using multiple modes of action where resistance risk is high. This content must be framed as a label-, registration-, crop-, target-, and local-guidance-dependent stewardship strategy rather than a product recommendation.

## Authoring blueprint for Module 33

Module 33 should present responsible pesticide use as a constrained, lawful part of IPM. The first lesson should teach label literacy, target fit, active ingredient and mode of action, hazard communication, and the hierarchy of product selection. The second lesson should teach personal protection, safe measuring and tank mixing, water-source safeguards, equipment checks, timing, weather, coverage, nozzle and calibration decisions, resistance prevention, hygiene, storage, and post-application review.

Likely assessment targets include interpreting a label before use; selecting safe mixing and measurement practices; choosing weather, nozzle, and calibration actions that minimise drift and protect coverage; and deciding when a registered, selective, label-directed product and resistance-management plan may be justified within IPM.
