## Key findings captured from pages 1–5

The document title is **Insect Pests and Mites (Acari) Identification and Management** and its learning objectives emphasize three outcomes: understanding the two different insect life cycles, identifying damages caused by different insect pests, and proposing management and control methods.

The outline presents four instructional blocks: the life cycle of an insect and categories of insects, diagnostic work on insect-damaged crops, crop management for insect-pest control, and general pest-control recommendations.

The life-cycle section distinguishes **complete metamorphosis** from **incomplete metamorphosis**. Complete metamorphosis is shown as egg, larva, pupa, and adult, with examples that include flea beetle, squash beetle, caterpillars or worms, *Tuta absoluta*, *Liriomyza* leafminer, fruit fly, and thrips. Incomplete metamorphosis is presented as egg, small nymph, large nymph, and adult, with examples including whitefly, aphids, cotton stainer, green leafhopper, and mealybugs.

The insect-category section links pest orders and damaging stages to field symptoms. A damage overview groups symptoms into sucking insects, miners and borers, and chewing insects. The slides note that sucking-insect injury may be difficult to evaluate visually because tissue damage can be limited while disease-transmission consequences remain important.

The diagnosis-and-management examples in the visible pages include the following crop-specific pests:

- **Fruit flies** on cucurbits and solanaceous crops, affecting fruits. Management points shown: regular monitoring, removal of damaged fruits away from the field, fruit protection with netting, attractants such as pheromones or lures or natural attractants, and spray use with botanical extracts such as neem extract or synthetic insecticides such as dinotefuran and spinosad, with reference to crop guides.
- **Leaf miner (*Liriomyza* sp.)** on cucurbits, solanaceous crops, legumes, allium, and okra, affecting leaves. Management points shown: regular monitoring, removal of affected plant parts, sticky traps for adults, and sprays using botanical extracts or synthetic insecticides such as dinotefuran and spinosad, with reference to crop guides.
- **Leaf miner moth (*Tuta absoluta*)** on tomato, affecting leaves and fruits. Management points shown: regular monitoring, removal of affected plants away from the field, use of black sticky traps near the soil surface, pheromone traps, light traps, and sprays matched to moth traps, microbial insecticides such as *Bacillus thuringiensis* and nucleopolyhedrosis virus when available, plus botanical extracts or synthetic insecticides such as spinosad, abamectin, or flubendiamide, with reference to crop guides.
- **Pod borer** on legumes, affecting pods. Management points shown: regular monitoring, removal of damaged pods away from the field, sticky traps, pheromone traps, light traps, and microbial insecticides such as *Bacillus thuringiensis* and nucleopolyhedrosis virus when available, plus botanical extracts or synthetic insecticides such as lambda-cyhalothrin, spinosad, abamectin, and chlorantraniliprole, with reference to crop guides.
- **Eggplant fruit and shoot borer** on solanaceous crops, affecting leaves and fruits. Management points shown: regular monitoring, removal of infested fruit and shoots away from the field, deep ploughing and harrowing to minimize pupation in soil, sticky traps, pheromone traps, light traps, microbial insecticides when available, and botanical extracts or synthetic insecticides such as lambda-cyhalothrin, spinosad, abamectin, and chlorantraniliprole, with reference to crop guides.
- **Leaf folder** on cucurbits, affecting leaves. Management points shown: regular monitoring, removal of affected plant parts and caterpillars, sticky traps, pheromone traps, light traps, microbial insecticides such as *Bacillus thuringiensis* and nucleopolyhedrosis virus, and botanical extracts or synthetic insecticides such as lambda-cyhalothrin, spinosad, abamectin, and chlorantraniliprole, with reference to crop guides.
- **Fruitworm** on cucurbits and solanaceous crops, affecting fruit and leaves. Management points shown: regular monitoring, reducing fruit-larva numbers to lower population pressure, deep ploughing to limit soil pupation, sticky traps, pheromone traps, light traps, microbial insecticides when available, and botanical extracts or synthetic insecticides such as lambda-cyhalothrin, spinosad, abamectin, and chlorantraniliprole, with reference to crop guides.

## Preliminary implications for Module 31

The first lesson can likely centre on life cycle, damaging stage, feeding category, and symptom-based diagnosis. The second lesson can likely centre on crop-safe management logic: monitoring, sanitation, trapping, exclusion, biological products, botanicals, label-directed synthetic options, and crop-guide compliance.

- **Whitefly** is shown on cucurbits, solanaceous crops, legumes, and okra, affecting leaves. Management points shown: regular monitoring, weed control to destroy host plants, use of reflective plastic mulch to reduce population, netting to exclude vectors, and sprays using neem extract or synthetic options such as lambda-cyhalothrin, dinotefuran, or thiocyclam oxalate. - **Aphids** are shown on cucurbits, solanaceous crops, okra, papaya, and legumes, affecting leaves and flowers. - **Thrips** are shown on cucurbits, solanaceous crops, brassicas, and allium, affecting leaves and fruits. Management points shown: regular monitoring, reflective plastic mulch, blue sticky traps, uprooting whole plants if viral disease symptoms are present, and sprays using neem extract or synthetic options such as lambda-cyhalothrin, dinotefuran, or thiocyclam oxalate. The visual examples tie thrips to onion, watermelon, TSWV, and WSMoV contexts.

The crop-management section frames **IPM** across several pillars. The visible table names cultural control, mechanical control, physical control, biological control, and resistance of the host plant. Cultural examples shown include crop rotation, intercropping, mulch, weed management, destruction of virus-infected and insect-infested plants or plant parts away from the field, avoidance of wet-condition work, and end-of-cycle crop-residue removal to prevent reinfestation. Mechanical and physical examples shown include pheromone traps, sticky traps, light traps, protective structures such as net tunnels, fruit bagging, soil solarisation, and direct removal of adults, larvae, and pupae by hand.

## Updated implications for Module 31

The module should distinguish at least three diagnostic logics: chewing or boring injury, sap-sucking or vector injury, and mite injury. It should also teach that management choices differ by pest biology, damaging stage, crop part affected, and virus-vector risk, while still following a unified IPM sequence of monitoring, sanitation, exclusion or trapping, biological or botanical tools, and crop-guide or label-directed synthetic use when necessary.

## Final findings captured from pages 11–14

The closing section expands mechanical, biological, botanical, and general-management guidance.

Mechanical control is illustrated with **pheromone traps** for fruit flies and **light traps**. One slide specifies a light-trap bait example using molasses or jaggery sugar and vinegar in a 1:1 ratio in an open bottle hung in the field. Another note warns that sweet traps, sticky traps, and light traps can also catch beneficial insects, and states that where pheromone traps are available, combining them with sweet, sticky, or light traps can improve target capture while reducing harm to beneficials.

The biological-control table emphasizes using natural enemies to keep pest populations at tolerable levels. It groups tools into parasitoids, predators, and insect pathogens. Visible examples include parasitoids such as *Trichogramma* and *Diadegma* wasps, predators such as lady beetles, spiders, and earwigs, and entomopathogens such as NPV and *Beauveria*. Product examples shown elsewhere include *Bacillus thuringiensis*, *Trichoderma harzianum*, PGPR formulations, and *Metarhizium anisopliae* plus *Beauveria bassiana*. The slides also point learners to the Koppert product range and the CABI BioProtection Portal for locally available products.

The biopesticides table includes several categories: EM5 as an insect repellent, botanical sprays as insect repellents, neem extract as an insecticide, plant ash, and natural vinegar plus molasses as insect traps.

The botanical-spray tables provide examples rather than a single universal recipe. The visible entries include:

- **Turmeric rhizome** preparations for aphids, armyworm, cotton semi-looper, green leaf hopper, mites, rice leaf roller, and rice stem borer.
- **Papaya leaves** preparations for caterpillars, flower thrips, and fruit flies.
- **Lemongrass roots** preparations for most agricultural pests.
- **Onion bulbs** preparations for ants, scales, spider mites, thrips, and aphids.
- **Garlic bulbs** preparations for caterpillars, armyworm, sucking insects, plant sap insects, and mealybugs.

The plant-ash table includes examples such as ash-chili powder for corn stalk borer, rice hull ash or eucalyptus or cypress wood ash for cutworm, snail, slug, turnip moth, and plant ash barriers for cutworms and fly maggots.

The general pest-management recommendations are explicit and suitable for source-grounded module phrasing. They instruct learners to scout for pest presence on growing points, tips or shoots, and the underside of leaves; clean cultivation with no infested plant material, fruits, or debris in the field; avoid high density and maintain proper aeration and light distribution; plant repellent and attractant plants with the main crops to avoid pest population build-up and attract beneficial insects; install bird perches to encourage insect-feeding birds in the field; ensure correct insect-pest identification for timely control application; and use minimal synthetic pesticides while always adapting IPM.

The final references point learners to additional modules on arachnids identification and management and control, sucking insect pests identification and management and control, caterpillar pests identification and management and control, and beetle pests identification and management and control.

## Authoring blueprint for Module 31

Module 31 should present a first lesson on recognizing pest groups through metamorphosis, damaging stage, feeding pattern, crop part affected, and vector-linked symptoms. The second lesson should emphasise integrated management: sanitation, weed and residue control, spacing and aeration, reflective mulch, exclusion netting, sticky, pheromone, and light traps, hand removal, biological control agents, biopesticides and botanical sprays, and restrained, label-directed synthetic pesticide use only within IPM and crop-guide limits.

Likely assessment targets include: distinguishing complete versus incomplete metamorphosis for scouting; identifying why sucking pests need attention even when tissue damage looks limited; selecting vector-focused actions when viral symptoms appear; and choosing an IPM sequence that prioritises monitoring, sanitation, exclusion, biological or botanical options, and careful synthetic escalation.
