# Expert Blueprint for the Next Crop-Advisor Content Upgrades

**Purpose.** This blueprint identifies the next high-value content layer for every current module. It does not replace the existing curriculum, assessments, competency map, Field Inquiry Studio, scenarios, field records, or safety safeguards. Instead, it proposes one deeper **field decision**, one **observable evidence artefact**, and one explicit **review or referral boundary** per module.

The design follows a simple progression: learners first observe and frame a field question, then compare evidence, make a bounded decision, record it, and review or refer when uncertainty remains. This aligns with the Farmer Field School emphasis on direct observation, discussion, local context, and learning-by-doing [1]. It also preserves the programme’s competency orientation toward usable skills, knowledge, and values for work [2]. Nutrient and soil content should continue to treat water, soil, crop, and nutrient management as an integrated system rather than isolated input choices [3].

> **Programme boundary.** No proposed activity supplies a universal fertiliser rate, pesticide recommendation, medical or exposure instruction, waste/disposal direction, legal reporting advice, or unverified local threshold. Where a decision is high consequence, the intended learning action is to verify a current label, laboratory result, authorised channel, extension advice, or specialist referral.

## The upgrade pattern for every module

| Layer | Addition | Learner outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Field decision | One authentic choice with incomplete information | The learner practises deciding what can be concluded and what still needs checking. |
| Evidence artefact | A concise map, table, log, photo annotation, comparison, or calculation record | Reasoning becomes inspectable by the learner, peer, or supervisor. |
| Review trigger | A stated observation, uncertainty, or risk that changes the next step | Learners learn not to overclaim and can explain when referral is appropriate. |

## Module-by-module additions

| Module | Expert content addition | Interactive evidence task | Review / referral boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **01. Advisory practice** | Add a **first-visit advisory protocol**: clarify the grower’s question, production goal, constraints, consent/confidentiality, and the decision that actually needs to be made. | Role-play an opening conversation; create a one-page issue brief separating reported symptoms from observed evidence. | Refer or pause when the grower’s requested action is outside the adviser’s evidence, authority, or competence. |
| **02. Soil and nutrition** | Add a **root-zone and nutrient-context interpretation lab** that connects texture, structure, rooting, water movement, pH, and nutrient availability. | Compare two field zones using a soil-profile observation sheet and write competing explanations for uneven growth. | Do not infer a nutrient deficiency from a symptom alone; use representative soil, plant, or laboratory evidence. |
| **03. Crop observation** | Add a **repeatable crop-walk protocol** covering transect choice, healthy-versus-affected comparison, field pattern, crop stage, and photo discipline. | Build a geoless field sketch with observation points, pattern notes, and an uncertainty statement. | A photograph or one observation point is not a confirmed diagnosis; expand the sample or refer when patterns conflict. |
| **04. Vegetable production planning** | Add a **seasonal production-system canvas** linking field, labour, water, nursery timing, market outlet, transport, cost risk, and contingency. | Build a farm-specific production calendar with three foreseeable constraints and a response trigger for each. | Do not treat a calendar as a guarantee; confirm current weather, water, labour, market, and local constraints before implementation. |
| **05. Cost planning and decisions** | Add a **partial-budget and sensitivity exercise** to distinguish a cost, an expected benefit, a cash-flow timing issue, and a field-risk assumption. | Compare two locally supplied, learner-entered options using an editable cost-and-risk table; explain the assumption that matters most. | The task is decision literacy, not investment or price advice; use current local costs and seek business support for consequential commitments. |
| **06. Crop and variety selection** | Add a **crop-and-variety fit matrix** using local climate, soil, water, crop-cycle length, disease history, market specification, seed source, and labour. | Score two candidate crops or varieties from verified local information and justify the trade-off. | Do not present a variety as universally suitable; validate seed status, local performance, buyer requirements, and production constraints. |
| **07. Factors affecting crop yield** | Add a **yield-limitation systems map** that distinguishes direct causes, contributing conditions, and indicators from causes. | Build a causal map for a yield gap and mark the three highest-value observations to make next. | Avoid single-cause claims; test the most plausible limiting factors before recommending a change. |
| **08. Climatic factors and crop yield** | Add a **weather-to-crop-stage risk rehearsal** covering heat, cold, wind, rainfall, humidity, and timing of sensitive operations. | Use a current weather observation and crop-stage note to create a short monitor–protect–recheck plan. | Do not make a seasonal forecast or blanket response; follow current local meteorological and extension information. |
| **09. Topographic factors and crop yield** | Add a **field-flow and access sketch** showing slope, runoff pathways, compacted traffic zones, drainage exits, and safe access. | Annotate a field map with water-flow observations and a low-risk monitoring point. | Structural drainage, erosion-control, or earthmoving decisions require locally competent design support. |
| **10. Edaphic soil factors and crop yield** | Add a **soil-profile observation protocol** for depth, structure, roots, compaction, moisture, stones, and layer changes. | Compare two soil pits or simulated profiles and write a crop-root implication without naming a treatment. | Field observations cannot replace laboratory analysis where chemistry, salinity, or contamination is uncertain. |
| **11. Soil degradation and management** | Add a **degradation-risk timeline** that connects bare soil, rainfall/runoff, compaction, organic-matter loss, and management history. | Assess a simulated field using cover, infiltration, root, runoff, and erosion cues; select a prevention-first next evidence step. | Do not promise soil recovery from one practice; verify site condition and seek support for severe erosion or contamination concerns. |
| **12. Nutrients required in plant nutrition** | Add a **nutrient-function versus symptom-evidence sorting activity** so learners distinguish function, deficiency-like symptom, mobility pattern, and competing explanation. | Match plant observations with the additional evidence needed before considering a nutrient decision. | Symptoms are prompts for investigation, not proof of deficiency or permission to apply a product. |
| **13. Nutrient management** | Add a **whole-farm nutrient-flow map** integrating soil supply, residues, organic resources, mineral inputs, crop removal, losses, and water management. | Map a nutrient pathway and identify the evidence needed to avoid loss or imbalance. | Do not optimise one nutrient in isolation; confirm pH, root condition, water, soil supply, and interactions. |
| **14. Acid soil causes and management** | Add a **pH interpretation and zone-comparison case** that distinguishes an observed crop problem from a verified acidity issue. | Assemble a sample-to-laboratory request, field history, and pH-result interpretation brief. | Amendment type, rate, timing, and incorporation are locally dependent high-consequence decisions requiring current laboratory and authorised technical guidance. |
| **15. How to promote soil health** | Add a **soil-health evidence dashboard** with physical, biological, chemical, water, and management-history indicators. | Record baseline indicators and choose one realistic observation interval for a management comparison. | Avoid treating one indicator as overall soil health; compare trends over time and maintain local context. |
| **16. Collect soil samples for soil testing** | Add a **sample-chain quality audit** covering zone definition, depth, clean tools, composite method, labelling, history, and the decision question. | Find deliberate errors in a simulated sampling plan, then produce a corrected chain-of-custody sheet. | Resample or seek laboratory support when zones are mixed, labels are incomplete, contamination is possible, or the result conflicts with field evidence. |
| **17. Nursery for vegetable production** | Add a **seedling-batch acceptance protocol** linking uniformity, root form, sanitation, vigour, growing-medium condition, and traceability. | Evaluate a simulated tray batch as accept, hold, or investigate, with evidence for the decision. | Do not transplant poor-quality or uncertain batches without checking the cause and crop-health risk. |
| **18. Open-field seedling production** | Add a **bed-condition and emergence monitoring diary** covering drainage, spacing, moisture, shade, uniform emergence, and loss pattern. | Compare two seedling-bed observations and select the next observation that would distinguish the explanations. | Avoid copying timing or density from another field; verify local weather, bed condition, seed lot, and crop guidance. |
| **19. Protective and cellular seedling production** | Add a **protected-nursery systems audit** for hygiene, tray condition, airflow, light, water, exclusion barriers, and crop uniformity. | Complete a risk-ranked inspection and create a corrective-monitoring sequence. | Disease, contamination, or persistent uneven growth requires verified diagnosis and appropriate nursery or extension support. |
| **20. Seedling production planning** | Add a **batch-to-field coordination simulator** that links sowing window, germination, hardening, field readiness, labour, weather, and contingency. | Draft two contingencies for delayed field readiness and explain the evidence that activates each. | Do not force a batch into the field because of calendar pressure; confirm plant and field readiness. |
| **21. Field preparation, mulching, and trellising** | Add a **field-layout rehearsal** for beds, access, mulch, trellis alignment, drainage, irrigation routing, and maintenance access. | Mark a layout on a field sketch and identify one runoff, access, or crop-support risk to recheck. | Verify local safety, materials, slope, wind, and crop-support needs before construction or major layout changes. |
| **22. Transplanting** | Add a **transplant acceptance and establishment check** covering root condition, planting depth, soil moisture, spacing, handling, and early-loss monitoring. | Use a photo or simulated row to identify evidence of acceptable establishment and a recheck date. | Do not use a transplant checklist as a substitute for plant-health verification or local establishment guidance. |
| **23. Water management** | Add a **root-zone water-and-air decision laboratory** that connects field observation, irrigation/rain history, soil texture, drainage, crop stage, and root condition. | Compare a dry and a saturated root-zone case; write the next evidence step rather than a fixed irrigation instruction. | Water-source safety, salinity, contamination, drainage design, and persistent stress require laboratory, authority, irrigation, or extension support. |
| **24. Irrigation systems** | Add an **irrigation-system fit matrix** linking water source, field zones, filtration, pressure, labour, energy, maintenance capacity, and crop needs. | Compare two system concepts using learner-entered local constraints and identify missing design information. | Do not produce a system design, pump specification, or operating setting without qualified local design support. |
| **25. Drip irrigation system** | Add a **uniformity investigation sequence** from source and filter through pressure, laterals, emitters, wetting pattern, and far-end observation. | Record near-to-far observations and choose the first maintenance or referral question to resolve. | Do not extend runtime or alter pressure before verifying distribution, source, filtration, and system condition. |
| **26. Fertilisation of vegetables** | Extend the new **Fertilisation Planning Lab** with a supervisor-reviewed case that tests label interpretation, nutrient-basis checks, field-zone logic, and 4R review triggers. | Submit a transparent draft from verified learner inputs, then compare it with supervisor feedback or a model evidence chain. | The lab must remain non-prescriptive: no universal rate, product choice, blend optimisation, or application concentration. |
| **27. Field care and maintenance** | Add a **weekly crop-care prioritisation board** covering crop stage, canopy, support, weeds, irrigation hardware, nutrient-plan review, sanitation, labour, and weather. | Triage a simulated week into urgent observation, planned maintenance, and defer/refer tasks with reasons. | Do not treat a routine as proof that the crop is safe; escalate unusual patterns, worker risks, or unresolved damage. |
| **28. Harvesting and post-harvest handling** | Add a **quality-and-loss pathway map** from maturity assessment through harvest handling, shade, container hygiene, sorting, packing, transport, and records. | Compare handling sequences and identify where damage, contamination, temperature, or delay risk enters. | Follow current food-safety, buyer, and local handling requirements; do not certify produce safety from a classroom exercise. |
| **29. Field diagnosis in vegetable crops** | Add a **differential-diagnosis board** that separates crop history, distribution pattern, plant part, roots, soil, water, weather, and management change. | Build three competing explanations and identify the fastest safe evidence step for each. | A diagnosis stays provisional until supported; use laboratory, extension, or specialist referral for uncertain, emerging, or consequential cases. |
| **30. Disease identification and management** | Add a **disease-evidence chain** covering host, symptom pattern, signs where applicable, crop stage, weather/moisture context, spread pattern, and alternative explanations. | Compare two look-alike disease scenarios and select sampling, isolation, or referral evidence—not a product response. | Do not provide treatment, product, disposal, or reporting instructions without current authorised guidance. |
| **31. Insect pests and mites identification and management** | Add a **scouting-and-beneficials comparison** linking injury pattern, life stage, crop stage, natural enemies, sampling route, and uncertainty. | Annotate a simulated scouting sheet with what was seen, what was not seen, and what needs confirmation. | Do not identify or control from one image; follow current extension or authorised pest-identification channels for uncertain cases. |
| **32. Integrated pest management** | Add an **IPM decision ladder** moving from prevention and monitoring to evidence-led intervention review, with economic, ecological, worker, water, and beneficial-organism considerations. | Build an IPM plan around a field scenario and identify the observation that could justify escalation or de-escalation. | Action thresholds, product decisions, and local regulations must come from current authorised local guidance, not generic course content. |
| **33. Responsible use of pesticides** | Add a **stop-work and label-literacy simulation** focusing on legal crop/target fit, current label status, equipment condition, weather, water protection, PPE, and emergency/referral prompts. | Decide whether a simulated operation must stop, seek clarification, or proceed only under verified label direction. | Never convert the course into a mixing, dosing, cleanup, medical, disposal, or reporting guide; use current labels and competent authorities. |
| **34. Weed management** | Add a **weed-pressure mapping and integrated-prevention plan** linking identification confidence, crop stage, field pattern, seedbank risk, rotation, cultivation, mulching, and recheck. | Map a simulated field and compare prevention, mechanical, cultural, and authorised escalation options at the evidence level. | Do not recommend a control product or rate without verified weed identity, crop fit, current label, and local authorised advice. |

## Recommended implementation waves

| Wave | Modules | Why this comes first | Deliverable pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wave 1: Field evidence and high-consequence judgement** | 03, 16, 23, 25, 26, 29–34 | These modules most directly determine whether a graduate can observe correctly, avoid overclaiming, and refer before an unsafe or costly action. | Add a short simulation, structured evidence record, and referral checkpoint per module. |
| **Wave 2: Establishment, resource use, and production reliability** | 04–06, 17–22, 24, 27–28 | These modules turn planning into crop establishment, resource stewardship, reliable maintenance, and marketable quality. | Add planning canvases, acceptance checks, field-layout exercises, and calendar-based review tasks. |
| **Wave 3: Soil and nutrient systems mastery** | 02, 07–15 | These modules build the diagnostic foundations that prevent superficial symptom-based decisions. | Add comparative soil/field cases, evidence dashboards, nutrient-flow maps, and laboratory interpretation checkpoints. |
| **Wave 4: Adviser professionalism and integration** | 01 plus cross-module capstones | The programme should show that technical advice is communication, ethics, decision traceability, and follow-through. | Add client-intake role play, case conference prompts, and integrated farm cases linking soil, water, crop protection, cost, and communication. |

## Quality controls for the build phase

Each future upgrade should meet the following acceptance tests:

1. It is **field-observable**: the learner can identify what to look for, compare, record, or calculate.
2. It is **evidence-bounded**: the activity distinguishes evidence, interpretation, uncertainty, and next action.
3. It is **locally adaptable**: crop guide, soil test, label, weather, price, or market values are entered or verified locally rather than hard-coded as universal.
4. It is **non-gating unless explicitly designated**: practice tools do not alter the 80% assessment rule, sequential gates, competency scoring, certification, or owner alerts.
5. It is **safe by design**: high-consequence content routes learners to current labels, laboratory evidence, authorised channels, extension, or specialist support.
