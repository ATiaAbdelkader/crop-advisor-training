## Key findings captured from pages 1–5

The document title is **Integrated Pest Management (IPM)**. Its stated learning objectives are to differentiate the goals and elements of IPM, identify and explain the four different methods of IPM, and integrate IPM concepts and methods when helping farmers with pest problems in the field.

The outline presents seven instructional blocks: the IPM concept, guide questions in IPM, scouting the field, cultural methods in IPM, mechanical and physical methods in IPM, biological methods in IPM, and chemical methods in IPM.

The document defines **pest** broadly as any species, strain, or biotype of plant, animal, or pathogenic agent injurious to plants and plant products, materials, or environments, and notes that vectors of parasites or pathogens of human and animal disease and animals causing public-health nuisance are included in the wider FAO definition. For the module context, the visible slide states that insect pests, diseases, weeds, and other organisms damaging the vegetable crop are included within the working definition of pest.

The document defines **Integrated Pest Management (IPM)** as an ecosystem approach to crop production and protection that combines different management strategies and practices to grow healthy crops and minimize the use of pesticides. It further states that IPM emphasizes the growth of a healthy crop with the least possible disruption to agro-ecosystems and encourages natural pest-control mechanisms.

The importance slides state that IPM helps farmers conserve the environment, produce safe and quality crops, and maintain farm profitability. Another slide links IPM importance with pressure from global population, increasing food demand, the need to increase farmed area and productivity, and the constraint that pest problems cause heavy crop losses that lower yield and quality.

The visible elements slide presents IPM as multidisciplinary. The listed elements are agronomy or crop and soil science, climatology or weather trends and effects, entomology and acarology or insects both pests and beneficial and mites (Acari), plant pathology for plant diseases, and economics for the cost-effectiveness of management methods. The slide also states explicitly that IPM is a process of decision making.

The visible components slide divides IPM into **prevention**, **monitoring**, and **intervention**. Prevention includes understanding conditions, selecting varieties, crop management, and appropriate hygiene measures. Monitoring includes inspecting fields, identifying issues, and determining action. Intervention includes choosing a method, planning the approach, and intervening responsibly.

The guide-questions slides present decision prompts that are suitable for module authoring. The visible prompts include: what is normal and what is the problem, with a reminder that proper identification and knowledge of the cause are critical; what is the problem and what could occur with normal situation, with prevention as the best approach through crop management and preventive treatment; how do the pests attack and what do they attack, with attention to leaves, fruits, stem, roots, and whole plants; checking surrounding cultivated plants; and looking for patterns in the field such as random distribution, a single row, or borders versus row interiors.

The document links IPM to **Integrated Crop Management (ICM)**, described as a holistic approach to crop production that addresses soil, water, nutrients, pests or diseases, and post-harvest management, taking measures to optimize crop growth while minimizing production in the short and long term. The visible ICM practices include good soil cultivation, healthy seedlings, correct spacing, maintaining good soil moisture, avoiding waterlogging, correct use of fertilizers and pesticides, removing old crops and weeds to prevent infestation spread, starting with healthy seedlings, direct sowing or thin sowing, good planting distance, raised beds, protected shelter, mulching, trellising, and good nutrition and water management.

The field-approach slides show an observation-analysis-decision-making cycle. Visible field instructions include scouting the field differently every time, checking a minimum number of plants in a spot with problems across leaf, stem, flower, fruit, roots, and soil, recording observations in a simple quantitative way, and collecting samples when needed. A scouting exercise asks learners to use insect nets and jars, paper and pencils, scout and record observations, map problems in the field with description and plant damage, collect or draw the insect present for identification, identify environmental conditions such as water, temperature, spacing, soil, weeds, and diseases, and draw the plant with its environment and comments.

The methods slide lists five IPM methods: cultural control, physical control, mechanical control, biological control, and chemical control. It explicitly states that **chemical control is always the last solution** and should be avoided if possible.

## Preliminary implications for Module 32

The first lesson can likely focus on IPM as an ecosystem-based decision process built around prevention, monitoring, intervention, field scouting, and multidisciplinary evidence. The second lesson can likely focus on applying the method hierarchy through integrated crop management, cultural, physical, mechanical, biological, and last-resort chemical actions selected from field diagnosis and pest pressure.

## Additional findings captured from pages 6–10

The methods section begins with **cultural control**, defined as practices that reduce pest establishment, reproduction, dispersal, and survival. Visible examples are soil preparation and site selection, use of resistant or tolerant varieties, crop rotation, intercropping, and influencing microclimate. The microclimate slide makes this practical through pruning, thinning, mulching, watering or draining, raised beds, and spacing.

The document then presents **mechanical and physical controls** as actions that kill a pest directly, block pests out, or make the environment unsuitable for it. The visible examples include yellow sticky traps for low-flying insects, light traps in the field at night to collect moths and other flying insects, scouting the field and collecting and destroying pests in small areas, and using protective structures such as net tunnels on the crop.

Pheromone traps are specifically named for fruit flies, *Tuta absoluta*, and other Lepidoptera, with a note to check what is available in the local country for the specific insect pest. The document also gives a sweet-trap example from North Thailand using white plastic bottles with side openings, a molasses mixture, and field placement on bamboo sticks at defined height and spacing. A related slide shows how to make a trap bottle and indicates the use of a lure, water with soap to break surface tension, and hanging hardware.

The caution slide states that sweet traps, sticky traps, and light traps can also trap beneficial insects. It further states that where pheromone traps are available, a combination with sweet, sticky, or light traps can improve efficiency and ensure that the insect pest target is captured more often and beneficials are captured less often.

The physical-control slides include **solarization** and **biofumigation**. Solarization is described with clear plastic, moist but not waterlogged soil before application, and at least six weeks or up to three to four months of field coverage. The slide states that clear plastic allows sunlight to pass through and heat the soil to temperatures lethal to many pests, weeds, soil-borne pathogens, and insect larvae, while also warning that solarization can share the same problem as traps by killing beneficial organisms. Biofumigation is illustrated with mustard, radishes, broccoli, canola, and related plants, which are incorporated before full bloom while fresh, with sufficient moisture before incorporation and an ensuing volatile compound release after maceration.

The **biological control** slide lists introducing, inviting, or establishing insect and mite predators or parasites, using botanical control such as trap crops or insect repellents, botanical pesticides, and biocontrol agents. A visible example table of biopesticides lists EM5, botanical sprays, neem extract, plant ash, Bordeaux mixture, soap, and oil with differing functions including insect repellent, disease control, insecticidal action, or broader soft-control roles.

The EM5 slide describes it as a preventive non-toxic insect repellent and gives a preparation structure using concentrated EM, molasses, natural vinegar, alcohol, water, and plant materials such as garlic, chili pepper, and flowers.

The botanical-spray slides state advantages and limitations. Advantages include lower cost, reduced harm to the environment, and local availability with easy preparation. Disadvantages include variability in results and the warning that some plants can be toxic to humans, so personal protective equipment should be used during handling.

Visible botanical examples against insect pests include garlic or kakawate and madre de cacao, turmeric, papaya, lemongrass, onion, and garlic, with pest examples such as worms, ants, caterpillars, flower thrips, fruit flies, spider mites, and sucking insects. A disease-focused botanical slide is also visible, suggesting that the source positions IPM across multiple pest categories rather than insects alone.

The biocontrol-agent slide features **Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt)**, describing it as the common name for a microbial insecticide with active protein crystals from a soil bacterium that is used mostly against larval caterpillars such as diamondback moth, imported cabbageworm, cabbage looper, cluster caterpillars or armyworms, and tomato fruitworm. Visible guidelines include observing the field twice per week, applying Bt in the late afternoon, using the spray underside of leaves, using good-quality nozzles, targeting small newly hatched caterpillars, and alternating Bt after two to three sprayings with another botanical spray.

## Updated implications for Module 32

The module should show IPM as a choice architecture rather than a single control method. It should teach learners to move from prevention and microclimate management, to monitoring and identification, to targeted physical, mechanical, biological, or botanical actions, while recognising side effects such as beneficial-insect capture and keeping chemical control as the last and most restricted step.

## Additional findings captured from pages 11–15

The biological-control sequence expands the source’s treatment of natural enemies and pathogens. One slide on **predators and parasitoids** tells learners to know beneficial insects and understand their role, and to avoid chemical pesticides that will kill natural enemies of pests.

The predator slides define predators as organisms that kill and feed on several to many individual prey during their lifetime and note that predators usually attack different life stages of pests such as adults, eggs, and larvae. Visible example groups and target-prey relations include lacewings and hoverflies against aphids; ladybird beetles against aphids; dragonflies against leafhoppers and other flying insects; praying mantis against stem borers, grasshoppers, and green bugs; ants against crickets, grasshoppers, and other insect pests; assassin bugs and spined soldier bugs against Lepidoptera and coleopteran larvae; ground beetles against larvae of Lepidoptera, maggots, snails, and slugs; and spiders and predatory mites as additional beneficial predators.

The source defines a **parasitoid** as an insect whose larva lives as a parasite and eventually kills the host. It states that parasitoids usually belong to Hymenoptera and Diptera and that major hosts include aphids, whiteflies, leaf-mining flies, and eggs and larvae of moths. The slides distinguish egg parasitoids, larval parasitoids, and pupal parasitoids, and visually emphasise aphid parasitic wasp activity through the contrast between healthy and parasitised aphids.

Specific parasitoid examples shown in the source include **Encarsia formosa** for whiteflies and **Trichogramma spp.** as egg parasitoids. A diamondback-moth parasitoid sequence explains that some parasitoids deposit eggs inside DBM larvae so that the parasitoid larva grows and kills the host inside it, while other species, such as *Diadegma semiclausum*, form cocoons outside the dead or dying larva. The slides describe the adult parasitoid emerging from the cocoon and repeating the cycle where hosts remain available.

The document then asks **what pathogens are**, describing them as microorganisms that can infect and kill the host and can enter through the skin or digestive system. Visible example groups are fungi, bacteria, virus, and viroid.

The entomopathogenic slide identifies fungi, bacteria, and virus options and links several examples to target pests. Visible entries include *Beauveria bassiana* and *Metarhizium anisopliae* against Lepidoptera and coleopteran pests, with *Beauveria* also shown against whiteflies, and *Akanthomyces muscarius* against aphids, whiteflies, thrips, and mealybugs. NPV is also shown for Lepidoptera targets. These slides reinforce that microbial tools in IPM are specific tools with target ranges rather than universal solutions.

## Updated authoring implications

Module 32 should not present biological control narrowly as a purchased product. It should teach learners to identify and conserve natural enemies, understand prey or host relationships, avoid unnecessary pesticide disruption, and use parasitoids or entomopathogens according to target pest biology and life stage.

## Final findings captured from pages 16–19

The closing slides extend biological control to additional market-ready resources. One slide adds **entomopathogenic nematodes**, with *Steinernema feltiae* shown as an example, while two resource slides direct learners to commercial and public product-discovery channels: the **Koppert** product range and the **CABI BioProtection Portal**, both framed as places to check what biological products are available in the learner’s country.

The **chemical control** section is explicit that this method carries important disadvantages. The visible disadvantages listed are residual effects on soil that degrade soil life, destruction of beneficial insects, residual effects on vegetables, environmental pollution, danger at home, and high cost. This reinforces the earlier instruction that chemical control is the last solution and should be avoided if possible.

When choosing a chemical control, the document instructs learners to consider whether the product is registered, which product will be most effective against the target pest, what side effects it may cause, and whether it is safe for the user and the consumer. Another slide advises choosing chemicals that are safer to use, selective instead of broad-spectrum, maintain the balance of beneficial insects, and are more effective in pest control, concluding that it is better to use less toxic products for good health and a healthy environment.

The pesticide-stewardship slide tells learners to check whether a pest is still controlled by the product, how often it is recommended, dose and **pre-harvest interval (PHI)**, hazard class, safety advice, protective clothing, environmental precautions, storage, emergency first aid procedures, and expiry date. A further slide on responsible use states that pesticide use should increase only by applying products when they are needed, applying effectively when they are needed, weighing and applying alternative treatments wisely, and evaluating the effectiveness of IPM methods implemented for future reference.

The source provides a short **self-evaluation of IPM actions** with five reflective questions: whether the problem was really serious, whether the actions were really necessary, whether the method worked as expected, whether the problem could be handled differently next time, and whether more information is needed to make the decision.

The closing summary defines IPM through four linked ideas: **observation**, **analysis**, **decision making**, and understanding and respecting the **agroecosystem**. It also states that IPM is not a fixed package of recommendations for any crop. A final reflection explains that integrated pest management is not only about knowing best strategies or involving advisors; it is also about farmer knowledge, decisions, community action, and integrating people’s mindset.

## Authoring blueprint for Module 32

Module 32 should present IPM as a repeatable advisory process rather than a menu of isolated tactics. The first lesson should teach the logic of prevention, monitoring, guide questions, scouting, and multidisciplinary field interpretation. The second lesson should teach how to combine cultural, physical, mechanical, biological, and carefully constrained chemical control while protecting beneficials, respecting product registration and safety requirements, and reviewing whether the action was justified.

Likely assessment targets include distinguishing IPM from routine pesticide use, selecting scouting and guide-question sequences, identifying when biological or physical methods should be preferred, explaining why chemicals are last-resort tools, and recognising that post-action self-evaluation is part of responsible IPM rather than an optional extra.
